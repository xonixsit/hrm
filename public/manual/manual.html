                TYPE OF FILING STATUS                  

In the context of U.S. income taxes, filing status refers to the category under which you file your federal income tax return. Your filing status affects your tax rate, eligibility for certain deductions and credits, and the overall calculation of your taxes. Here are the primary types of filing statuses.

Single (S) -

  • You must file as single if you are unmarried.

Single filers include people who are unmarried or legally separated from a spouse under a divorce or separate maintenance decree on the last day of the year and who do not qualify for another filing status.

Married Filing Jointly (MFJ) -

If you are married, TP and SP agree to file together. On a joint return, you need to report your combined income and deduct your combined allowable expenses.

Married filing jointly is a tax filing status that allows a married couple to file a single tax return that records both of their taxable income, deductions, credits, and exemptions.

Married filing jointly is generally a better choice for couples, as it makes them eligible for some advantageous tax credits and deductions. 

Married couples filing jointly generally have access to more tax benefits.

When using the married filing jointly filing status, both spouses are equally responsible for the return and the taxes. If either one understates the taxes due, both are equally liable for the penalties, unless the other spouse can prove they were unaware of the mistake and did not benefit from it.

Married Filing Separately (MFS) -

  • If TAXPAYERis married. This method may benefit to you if you want to be responsible only for your own tax If you and your spouse donot agree to file a joint return, you may have to use this filing status.

Married filing separately is a tax status for married couples who choose to record their respective incomes, exemptions, and deductions on separate tax returns.

This means both married spouses file separate tax returns. When one spouse has significant medical expenses or miscellaneous itemized deductions, or when both spouses have about the same amount of income, it might be wiser to file separately. The alternative to married filing separately is married filing jointly. It usually makes sense financially for married couples to file jointly.

 Head of Household (HOH) -

  • If TAXPAYER pay more than half of the home expenses and have more income with dependents can file for (HOH).

 You can file as head-of-household even if you’re married but if you have a spouse, it’s likely more beneficial tax-wise to file jointly. However, if you are filing separately, you can claim head-of-household status if you meet these three criteria:

  • Your spouse did not live with you for the last six months of the year.
  • You provided the main home of the qualifying child and paid for more than half the home costs.
  • You are claiming your child as a dependent.

It’s important to analyze the best outcome for your personal situation and that of your spouse if you are married before deciding on your filing status.

Claiming “head of household” as your filing status (versus filing as single or married filing separately) benefits you in two ways. First, you’ll get a lower tax rate.

                Purpose:

ITIN Credits

  • The ITIN Credit generally refers to the benefits available to taxpayers who use an Individual Taxpayer Identification Number (ITIN) rather than a Social Security Number (SSN) for tax purposes. Here’s an overview of how the ITIN impacts credits and what you need to know:
  •  Eligibility: For the Child Tax Credit, a qualifying child must have a valid SSN. If a taxpayer uses an ITIN, they generally cannot claim the Child Tax Credit for any children.  
  • Exception: The Credit for Other Dependents (which provides up to $500 per qualifying dependent) is available to taxpayers with ITINs, as long as the dependents have valid ITINs or SSNs.
